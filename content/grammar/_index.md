## How Japanese sentences work

Japanese is a **Subject–Object–Verb** language. The verb always goes at the end.

| English (SVO) | Japanese (SOV) |
|---------------|----------------|
| I eat sushi. | 私は寿司を**食べます**。|
| She reads books. | 彼女は本を**読みます**。|
| He studies Japanese. | 彼は日本語を**勉強します**。|

**Three things to get right:**

1. **Particles** — small words after nouns that show *how* that noun functions in the sentence (topic, subject, object, location, direction...)
2. **Verb forms** — verbs change their ending depending on tense, politeness, and grammatical function
3. **Patterns** — fixed structures like 〜たいです (want to do) or 〜ことができます (can do)

Master those three and you can build almost any Japanese sentence.

## Polite vs. casual Japanese

One of the first things to understand is that Japanese has two registers — **polite** (です・ます form) and **casual** (plain form) — and switching between them incorrectly is one of the most common beginner mistakes.

| Style | Used when | Example |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| Polite (丁寧語) | Strangers, shops, work, teachers | 食べます (*tabemasu*) — I eat |
| Casual (普通体) | Friends, family, close peers | 食べる (*taberu*) — I eat |

Start by learning the **polite form**. It's appropriate in almost every situation you'll encounter as a beginner in Japan — ordering food, asking for directions, talking to people you've just met. Once you're comfortable there, learn the casual form for conversations with friends.

## The most important verb form: て-form

If you only master one verb form beyond the basics, make it the **て-form** (te-form). It unlocks an enormous range of grammar patterns:

| Pattern | Meaning | Example |
|---------|---------|---------|
| 〜ている | ongoing action / state | 食べている — eating (right now) |
| 〜てください | please do ~ | 見てください — please look |
| 〜てもいい | it's okay to ~ | 入ってもいい — you can come in |
| 〜てみる | try doing ~ | 食べてみる — try eating it |
| 〜てから | after doing ~ | 食べてから — after eating |

る-verbs: drop る, add て (食べる → 食べて)  
う-verbs: the ending changes depending on the final sound (書く → 書いて、話す → 話して、飲む → 飲んで)

## Suggested learning order

If you're working through grammar from scratch, this order builds on itself naturally:

1. **Hiragana & katakana** — read everything in kana first, avoid romaji dependency
2. **Basic sentence structure** — は、が、を、に、で particles + verb endings (〜です、〜ます)
3. **Adjective types** — い-adjectives (寒い) and な-adjectives (元気な) behave differently
4. **て-form** — opens up a huge range of patterns all at once
5. **Past tense** — た-form (食べた) follows similar rules to て-form
6. **Want / can / must** — たいです、〜ことができます、なければなりません
7. **Relative clauses** — modifying nouns with verb phrases (食べた人 — the person who ate)

Don't try to memorise everything at once. Learn one pattern, find it in real Japanese (anime, songs, conversations), then move to the next.

## Common beginner mistakes

**Mixing up は and が** — both mark subjects but they're not interchangeable. は marks the *topic* of the sentence (what you're talking about). が marks the *subject* doing the action, or is used in new information. When in doubt, は for general statements, が for specific or contrastive ones.

**Forgetting particles entirely** — in English you rely on word order. In Japanese you rely on particles. Drop one and the sentence breaks. Treat them as part of the word, not an optional add-on.

**Using ない as a standalone negative** — ない on its own means "there isn't" (ない → nothing). To negate a verb politely you need 〜ません (食べません — I don't eat). ない is the plain form negative (食べない).

**Over-translating from English** — Japanese omits pronouns constantly. You don't need 私は (I) in every sentence. Say it once if context needs it, then drop it.
