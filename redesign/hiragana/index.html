Core 46

清音 46 characters

Tap any card to hear the pronunciation. All audio uses a native Japanese voice at a slightly reduced speed — good for getting the exact vowel shape right before you try to say it yourself.

Hiragana is a syllabic alphabet — each character represents a whole syllable, not a single consonant or vowel. Once you know all 46, you can read and write any word in Japanese. That makes it the most important thing to learn first.

Reading Guide

読み方のガイド

Japanese vowels sound different from English

The five vowels — a, i, u, e, o — are pure and consistent. They don't shift or blend the way English vowels do. Pronounce them the same every time, regardless of what's around them:

  • a — as in "father", never "cat"
  • i — as in "meet", short and clean
  • u — lips slightly forward but not rounded, like "doo" without the rounding
  • e — as in "bet", never a long "ay" sound
  • o — as in "go", pure and short

Long vowels

A vowel followed by the same vowel (or in the case of o, sometimes followed by う) is held for twice as long. This matters — different meanings can hinge entirely on vowel length. おばさん (obasan) means aunt; おばあさん (obaasan) means grandmother. The only difference is that long あ.

ん — the standalone N

Unlike every other hiragana character, ん stands alone as a consonant with no following vowel. It sounds slightly different depending on what comes after it — more like a full N before t/d/n sounds, closer to an M sound before b/p/m sounds, and more nasal before vowels or at the end of a word. You don't need to think about this consciously — it happens naturally once you start listening.

Double consonants (っ)

A small っ (small tsu) before a consonant doubles it. The doubled consonant isn't said twice — instead, there's a brief, silent pause before it. ざっし (zasshi, magazine) sounds like the speaker stops for a beat before the sh. Miss it and you might say a completely different word.

Particles は and へ

When used as particles (grammar markers, not standalone words), は is read wa, not ha, and へ is read e, not he. This is one of the first things that trips up new readers — the written form doesn't match the pronunciation in these specific cases.

FAQ

Most people can recognise all 46 characters within 1–2 weeks of daily practice. Being able to read them fluently — at a natural speed without pausing — takes another 2–4 weeks of regular reading. The best practice is to stop using romaji completely and just read real Japanese as soon as possible, even if it's slow at first.

Real Japanese uses all three scripts — hiragana, katakana, and kanji — mixed together in a single sentence. Hiragana handles grammar particles, verb endings, and words without common kanji. Adult text uses hiragana constantly. You can't read Japanese without it.
