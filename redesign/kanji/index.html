N3 Kanji

日本語能力試験 ~370 characters

N3 marks the transition into intermediate Japanese — newspaper headlines, short articles, and longer conversations become readable. The ~370 characters at this level include abstract nouns, formal vocabulary, and a wider range of compound words.

N2 Kanji

日本語能力試験 ~380 characters

N2 represents near-fluent reading ability. The ~380 additional characters cover formal writing, business language, literary vocabulary, and complex compound words encountered in native Japanese media.

N1 Kanji

日本語能力試験 600+ characters

N1 is the highest JLPT level. The 600+ additional characters include rare literary kanji, classical vocabulary, and highly formal or specialised terms. Reading native Japanese novels, news editorials, and academic texts requires N1-level kanji familiarity.

Reading Guide

漢字の読み方

On'yomi and kun'yomi

Every kanji has two types of reading. On'yomi (音読み) are Chinese-derived pronunciations — they're typically used in compound words (two or more kanji together). Kun'yomi (訓読み) are native Japanese pronunciations — they appear when a kanji stands alone or is followed by hiragana endings (okurigana).

  • 食べる — kun'yomi たべる, because 食 stands alone with a verb ending
  • 食事 — on'yomi しょく, because 食 and 事 form a compound (meal / dining)

A single kanji can have multiple readings of each type. 日 reads にち in 日曜日 (Sunday), にほん in 日本 (Japan), ひ in 日 (the sun), and か when counting days — 一日 (ついたち, first day of the month) is a completely irregular reading. Context and vocabulary are the only way through.

Radicals — the building blocks

Kanji are built from smaller components called radicals (部首, bushu). Many kanji share common radicals that hint at meaning. 海 (sea), 泳 (swim), and 湖 (lake) all contain the water radical 氵on the left. Recognising radicals helps you guess the rough meaning of unfamiliar kanji and makes them easier to remember as a group.

Stroke count and dictionary order

Traditional kanji dictionaries are indexed by radical and then by stroke count. The stroke count listed on each card above is the total strokes — including all components. Most digital tools use on'yomi readings for indexing, so knowing the on'yomi of common radicals helps when using paper references.

How many kanji do I need?

The Japanese government publishes a list of 2,136 kanji for everyday use (常用漢字, jōyō kanji). Japanese children learn all of them by the end of high school. N5 covers about 80, N4 about 250 total, N3 about 650, N2 about 1,000, and N1 about 2,000. For practical reading — news, social media, everyday signs — 1,500–2,000 is where things become comfortable.

FAQ

The main rule of thumb: on'yomi in compound words (two kanji together), kun'yomi when the kanji stands alone or has hiragana after it. 山 alone = やま. 富士山 = ふじさん (Fujisan). 山道 = やまみち — kun'yomi for both because it's a native compound. There are exceptions, but this rule handles the majority of cases. The rest comes from memorising vocabulary rather than rules.

Flashcard systems like Anki are effective for this. Learning "kanji lists" in order without vocabulary context is the slow route.
