N4 is the second level of the JLPT, sitting just above N5. It certifies that you can understand basic Japanese used in everyday situations: follow conversations on familiar topics spoken at near-natural speed, and read simple written material — texts on everyday subjects, short messages, and basic explanations.

Passing N4 is the point where Japanese starts to feel genuinely usable. You'll have enough vocabulary and grammar to get by in Japan, hold basic conversations with patient speakers, and understand the gist of everyday written Japanese. Most people reach N4 within six to twelve months of passing N5, or in twelve to eighteen months of starting from zero.

The vocabulary roughly doubles from N5 (~800 to ~1,500 words), the grammar increases in complexity with conditionals, passives, and verb conjugation forms, and the kanji jump from 80 to 300 characters. The test format is similar to N5 but the listening is faster and the reading passages are longer.

Key Grammar Areas

文法のポイント

N4 grammar introduces verb conjugation in earnest. The patterns below are the ones that appear most heavily in the test — getting these solid will handle the bulk of the grammar section.

PatternMeaningExample
〜ている
te-form + いる		 Ongoing state or habitual action — "is doing" / "has done" 日本語を勉強している。
I am studying Japanese.
〜てもいい
te-form + もいい		 Permission — "it's OK to…" / "you may…" ここに座ってもいいですか。
May I sit here?
〜なければならない
negative + ならない		 Obligation — "must…" / "have to…" もっと練習しなければならない。
I have to practise more.
〜られる
passive form		 Passive voice — "is/was done (to someone)" 先生に褒められた。
I was praised by the teacher.
〜させる
causative form		 Causative — "make/let someone do" 子供を寝させた。
I put the child to sleep.
〜たら
conditional (tara)		 Conditional — "if/when … then…" (past basis) 雨が降ったら、行きません。
If it rains, I won't go.
〜ば
conditional (ba)		 Conditional — "if…" (general / hypothetical) お金があれば、買います。
If I had money, I'd buy it.
〜ながら
stem + ながら		 Simultaneous actions — "while doing…" 音楽を聴きながら、勉強する。
I study while listening to music.
〜そうだ
stem + そうだ		 Appearance / hearsay — "looks like…" / "I heard that…" 雨が降りそうだ。
It looks like it's going to rain.
〜ようだ
plain form + ようだ		 Resemblance / inference — "it seems that…" / "looks as if…" 彼は疲れたようだ。
He seems tired.

You need at least 90 points total and at least 38 points in Language Knowledge (scaled), 19 in Reading, and 19 in Listening. A strong performance in one area cannot rescue a failed section. The pass rate for N4 sits around 38–42% — harder than N5, but well within reach with focused preparation.

Passing Tips

合格のコツ

Vocabulary doubles — review, don't just add

At N4 you need ~1,500 words, up from ~800 at N5. The trap is spending all your energy on new words and forgetting the N5 base. Mix new N4 vocabulary with daily review of N5 words you're not yet solid on. Spaced repetition (Anki, or any flashcard system) handles this automatically — set it up early.

Verb conjugation is the core skill

N4 grammar is dominated by verb forms: te-form, ta-form, potential, passive, causative, volitional, and the conditional forms ば and たら. These aren't isolated patterns — they're a system. If you understand the conjugation logic once, every grammar pattern built on it clicks into place. Learn the forms first, then the patterns on top.

Kanji: stop relying on furigana

At N5, most kanji appear with furigana. At N4, furigana becomes sparse — you're expected to know the readings. The 300 N4 kanji include common words you'll see everywhere: 時間、場所、仕事、気持ち、家族. Learn them in vocabulary context rather than in isolation; the kanji section rewards recognition in words, not abstract recall.

Listening: train at natural speed

The single biggest shock for N5 passers sitting N4 is the listening speed. N5 audio is slow and careful; N4 is close to natural conversation pace. Start listening to native material early — JDramas on Netflix, Japanese YouTube, NHK Web Easy — so fast speech stops feeling like a foreign language and starts feeling like Japanese spoken quickly.

Reading: learn to skim strategically

N4 reading passages are longer and the combined session with language knowledge is just 60 minutes. Read the question first, then scan the passage for the answer. You don't need to understand every word — you need to find the right detail or identify the writer's main point. Practice this skill with the mock tests before the exam day.

Grammar: prioritise high-frequency forms

Of the ~170 N4 grammar patterns, a subset accounts for the majority of test questions: ている, てもいい, なければならない, たら / ば conditionals, passive, and causative. Get those airtight first, then fill in the rest. The mock tests will quickly show you where your gaps are.

FAQ

Most learners who have passed N5 need 6–12 months of consistent study to reach N4 level. The Japan Foundation estimates approximately 300 hours total study for N4, compared to 150 hours for N5. If you study 1 hour a day from the point you passed N5, that's roughly 9 months of additional study. Some learners move faster with intensive study; others take longer if they're reinforcing N5 gaps at the same time.
More so than N5, yes — but with limits. With N4 level Japanese you can handle daily life in Japan fairly well: convenience stores, restaurants, directions, basic conversations with locals. You'll understand common signs and simple notices. For work or study, N4 is rarely sufficient — most Japanese companies require N2 or N1, and universities typically require N2 at minimum. N4 is the point where real-world Japanese stops being completely opaque, but N3 is where it starts being genuinely practical.
N5 reading uses very short texts (a few sentences each) on highly familiar topics with heavy furigana and simple vocabulary. N4 introduces mid-length passages on broader topics — explanations, instructions, short articles — with reduced furigana and more complex sentence structures. Questions also require more inference: at N5 you're mostly locating factual information; at N4 you're also asked to interpret the writer's intent or draw conclusions from multiple pieces of information in a passage.
N3 is often described as the hardest jump in the JLPT system. The vocabulary roughly doubles again (from ~1,500 to ~3,750 words), the grammar becomes significantly more complex, and — crucially — N3 reading uses near-authentic texts on everyday subjects. The listening includes longer monologues and more abstract topics. Most learners who passed N4 need 12–18 months of serious study to reach N3. It's the level where learners typically start consuming native Japanese media in earnest, which both requires and builds the skills N3 tests.
