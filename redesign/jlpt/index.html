Which level should I aim for?

レベル選択
Level What you can do Study time from zero
N5 Read hiragana & katakana, understand basic greetings and simple sentences 3–6 months
N4 Handle everyday situations, read simple texts with furigana 6–12 months
N3 Understand most daily conversation, read straightforward articles 1–2 years
N2 Read newspapers, follow complex conversation, work in Japanese 2–4 years
N1 Near-native reading and listening across all contexts 4–6+ years
Start with N5. Even if you feel confident, the vocabulary builds on itself — skipping levels means gaps you'll hit later. Most learners find N3 the hardest jump; the vocabulary load roughly doubles from N4. N2 is the practical threshold for working in Japanese.

Exam format

試験の構成

The JLPT has three sections at every level. You must hit the overall pass mark and meet the minimum per section — you can't compensate for a weak section with a strong one.

Section 1
言語知識
Vocabulary & Characters
Kanji readings, vocabulary in context, word formation, synonym selection. Tests whether you recognise and understand words — not just recall them.
Section 2
文法・読解
Grammar & Reading
Sentence grammar, sentence ordering, text-based grammar, and reading comprehension passages. Gets significantly longer at N2 and N1.
Section 3
聴解
Listening
Short dialogues and longer passages played once. Select the correct answer from images or written options. The official 公式問題集 workbooks include audio.

Exam dates

試験日程

The JLPT runs twice a year: first Sunday of July and first Sunday of December. Registration opens 3–4 months before each sitting.

In Japan
Register directly at jlpt.jp
Outside Japan
Via your country's local administration — Japan Foundation site lists every country
Register early. Spots fill fast in popular cities — London, New York, Sydney. Set a reminder for when registration opens or you risk missing a convenient venue.

Frequently asked questions

よくある質問
The JLPT (Japanese Language Proficiency Test / 日本語能力試験) is the world's most recognised Japanese language qualification, administered by the Japan Foundation and Japan Educational Exchanges and Services. Over 1.4 million candidates sit it annually across more than 80 countries. Five levels — N5 (beginner) through N1 (near-native) — testing vocabulary, kanji, grammar, reading, and listening.
Start with N5 if you've been studying for less than six months. N5 tests hiragana, katakana, around 800 vocabulary items, and basic sentence structure. If you've been studying for a year and can handle simple conversations, N4 may be a better target.
Most learners reach N5 readiness within 3–6 months studying 20–30 minutes a day. The total study hours typically quoted is 150–300 hours for someone with no prior Japanese. This varies depending on prior experience with Asian languages and how much natural exposure you get.
