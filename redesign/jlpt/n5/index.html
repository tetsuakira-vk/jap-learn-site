N5 is the entry level of the Japanese Language Proficiency Test (JLPT) — the world's most widely-taken Japanese language exam. It certifies that you can understand basic Japanese in limited everyday situations: simple conversations about familiar topics, short texts, and straightforward written material like signs and schedules.

N5 is a realistic goal for someone who has studied Japanese seriously for three to six months. You don't need to speak or write Japanese to pass — the test is entirely multiple choice (reading and listening). There's no pass/fail on individual sections; you need a total score above a threshold, with each section having a minimum mark.

Section minimums

You need at least 80 points total and at least 19 points in each individual section. A very high score in one section cannot compensate for failing another. The test is offered twice a year in Japan (July and December) and once a year in many countries.

Passing Tips

合格のコツ

Vocabulary is the highest return on time

N5 vocabulary (~800 words) is the single biggest contributor to your score. It feeds directly into the vocabulary section, the grammar section (where you need to understand the words in questions), and the listening section. Do vocabulary daily — 20 new words a day means the whole list in 40 days.

Learn hiragana and katakana first

If you haven't already, spend the first two weeks solidly on the two kana scripts. The test is written almost entirely in hiragana and katakana at N5 level — you cannot read the questions without them. The kanji appear with furigana at this level, but you need to be able to read their readings. Hiragana guide →

Grammar patterns over rules

Don't try to memorise grammar rules abstractly. Learn the ~100 N5 patterns as fixed phrases with example sentences, then vary them. は vs が, て-form,〜たい, 〜ている — learn each one with two or three examples and you'll recognise them in the test without needing to consciously apply a rule.

Listening: don't translate

The listening section moves quickly. If you try to translate everything you hear into English you'll fall behind. Train yourself to understand Japanese directly by listening to lots of native material at your level — the N5 mock tests on this site use the same format as the real exam.

Kanji: recognition, not writing

The JLPT does not test writing. You only need to recognise kanji and their readings. Focus on the 80 N5 kanji in reading context — see them in vocabulary (日本語、学校、先生) rather than memorising them in isolation. The vocabulary study naturally covers the kanji.

Time management on the day

The language knowledge and reading sections are combined. Many test-takers spend too long on grammar and run out of time for reading. Aim for 20 minutes on language knowledge, leaving 30 minutes for reading. If you're stuck on a question, mark your best guess and move on — there's no penalty for wrong answers.

N5 covers everyday survival Japanese — you can handle very basic situations, read simple signs, and follow short conversations on familiar topics. N4 adds the ability to understand basic Japanese in more varied everyday contexts.
