N2 is the professional and academic gateway of the JLPT. It certifies that you can understand Japanese used in a wide range of everyday and near-professional situations — read newspapers, follow TV news, understand complex written material on a variety of topics, and hold detailed conversations on abstract subjects.

N2 is the level that actually opens doors in Japan. Most universities require N2 for international students. Many Japanese companies accept N2 as sufficient for office work, customer service, and graduate roles — particularly for positions where you're working in both English and Japanese. A significant number of learners spend two or more years at N3 level before being ready for N2.

The vocabulary reaches ~6,000 words and the 1,000 kanji include the full Jōyō kanji that every Japanese elementary school graduate is expected to know. Grammar becomes formally complex — business register, literary constructions, and nuanced connectives that don't exist in lower levels.

Study Path

学習の流れ

At N2, immersion isn't optional — it's the only way to acquire 6,000 words and 1,000 kanji. Textbook study and immersion should run in parallel the entire time, with native input making up an increasing share of your study hours as you progress.

Key Grammar Areas

文法のポイント

N2 grammar is characterised by formal connective expressions — the kind found in editorials, business documents, and academic writing. These are rarely heard in casual speech but appear extensively in written Japanese and formal contexts.

PatternMeaningExample
〜にもかかわらず
noun/plain form + にもかかわらず		 Concession — "despite…" / "in spite of…" / "regardless of…" 雨にもかかわらず、大勢の人が集まった。
Despite the rain, a large crowd gathered.
〜に基づいて
noun + に基づいて		 Basis — "based on…" / "grounded in…" データに基づいて判断する。
I'll make a decision based on the data.
〜ざるを得ない
nai-stem + ざるを得ない		 Unavoidable necessity — "cannot help but…" / "have no choice but to…" この結果は認めざるを得ない。
I have no choice but to accept this result.
〜ものの
plain form + ものの		 Concession with limitation — "although…" / "even though… (but)" 合格したものの、自信はまだない。
Although I passed, I'm still not confident.
〜に過ぎない
noun/plain form + に過ぎない		 Minimising — "nothing more than…" / "merely…" / "only…" これは偶然に過ぎない。
This is nothing more than coincidence.
〜に加えて
noun + に加えて		 Addition — "in addition to…" / "on top of…" 経験に加えて、資格も必要だ。
In addition to experience, qualifications are also required.
〜につれて
plain form + につれて		 Parallel change — "as… (something changes), so does…" 時間が経つにつれて、記憶が薄れる。
As time passes, the memories fade.
〜をめぐって
noun + をめぐって		 Issue at centre — "concerning…" / "surrounding…" / "over (the issue of)…" その問題をめぐって、議論が続いている。
The debate continues surrounding that issue.
〜に反して
noun + に反して		 Contrary to expectation — "contrary to…" / "against…" 予想に反して、結果は良かった。
Contrary to expectations, the result was good.
〜にしたがって
noun/plain form + にしたがって		 Accordance or proportional change — "in accordance with…" / "as… increases, so does…" ルールにしたがって、行動してください。
Please act in accordance with the rules.

Test Format

試験の構成
Keigo is tested at N2. Formal and respectful speech forms (尊敬語 sonkeigo, 謙譲語 kenjōgo, 丁寧語 teineigo) appear in both the vocabulary and grammar sections. N2 is also where business-register grammar patterns begin appearing regularly in the test.

You need 90 total and at least 38 points in Language Knowledge, 19 in Reading, and 19 in Listening. The combined language knowledge and reading session is 105 minutes — managing time across vocabulary, grammar, and long reading passages in a single sitting is one of the distinctive challenges of N2. The pass rate is around 22–30%, reflecting that N2 requires a level of Japanese that takes sustained, multi-year effort.

Passing Tips

合格のコツ

Newspapers and editorials are your best reading practice

N2 reading passages mirror editorial writing: structured arguments, formal vocabulary, long complex sentences. The best daily practice is reading Japanese newspaper opinion pieces and articles — Asahi Shimbun, Mainichi Shimbun, or NHK News Web. You don't need to understand everything; the exposure to the register and sentence structures directly prepares you for what appears in the test.

Learn keigo as a system, not a list

Respectful language (尊敬語 / 謙譲語 / 丁寧語) appears explicitly in N2 vocabulary and grammar questions. Many learners try to memorise it as isolated pairs (言う → おっしゃる, 食べる → 召し上がる) and find it doesn't stick. Instead, understand the logic: sonkeigo elevates the subject, kenjōgo lowers the speaker. Once the principle is clear, the forms make structural sense and are far easier to recall.

Grammar: connective expressions in written Japanese

N2 grammar is dominated by formal connective expressions that barely exist in casual speech — にもかかわらず, をめぐって, に基づいて, ものの, ざるを得ない. The most effective way to internalise them is reading Japanese in contexts where they actually appear: news commentary, business writing, Wikipedia articles. Encounter them repeatedly in context and they become automatic; drilled in isolation, they're brittle under test pressure.

Listening: abstract topics at speed

N2 listening includes monologues on abstract topics — opinion pieces, explanations of social phenomena, news commentary. Unlike lower levels, the answers often require inference rather than direct information retrieval. Watch NHK news broadcasts and Japanese opinion shows. TED Talks in Japanese are also excellent — structured arguments on abstract topics at natural speed, very close to the N2 listening register.

Time management: the 105-minute section is the trap

The combined language knowledge and reading section lasts 105 minutes and covers vocabulary, grammar, and long reading passages. Many N2 test-takers run out of time before finishing the reading. Target 35–40 minutes on language knowledge questions and leave 65–70 minutes for reading. If you're slow on one grammar question, mark your best guess and move — the long passages at the end carry more marks.

Vocabulary: 6,000 words needs a system

You cannot brute-force 6,000 words with flashcards in a reasonable time. Build vocabulary from multiple sources simultaneously: an Anki deck for core N2 words, intentional reading of native texts, and shadow listening. The words you encounter in multiple contexts — a podcast, then a grammar example, then a news article — are the ones that actually transfer to test performance.

FAQ

Most learners need 18–24 months of consistent study after passing N3. The Japan Foundation estimates 600 hours total from scratch for N2. From N3, you're adding ~2,250 new vocabulary words, 350 more kanji, and a significant shift in grammar register. Learners who combine structured study with heavy immersion (reading native content daily, watching native Japanese regularly) often move faster; those who rely on textbooks alone tend to find the vocabulary acquisition too slow. Two years from N3 is a realistic, achievable timeline with 1–2 hours of study per day.
N2 is widely accepted for Japanese-language roles in international companies, customer service positions in Japan, teaching English in Japanese schools, and most administrative and office roles in Japanese companies. Many Japanese companies formally list N2 as the entry requirement for non-native speakers, particularly in sectors like IT, finance, manufacturing, and retail. That said, N2 is the floor, not the ceiling — in highly language-intensive roles (law, journalism, high-level business negotiation), N1 is expected. In practice, your actual language ability matters more than the certificate, but N2 gets you past the first screening at most companies.
It's complex. N2 certifies reading and listening comprehension at an upper-intermediate level — it doesn't test speaking or writing. Many N2 holders can read a newspaper and follow a news broadcast but struggle with spontaneous conversation or producing complex written Japanese. The test also doesn't assess accent or pronunciation. In terms of comprehension, N2 is broadly equivalent to B2 on the European CEFR scale — genuinely functional, but not effortless. Learners who supplement JLPT study with speaking practice (language partners, conversation lessons) typically find N2 level Japanese is enough to live and work in Japan comfortably.
N1 tests comprehension of complex Japanese across virtually all domains — including academic writing, literary prose, business documents, and classical literary forms. The vocabulary expands to ~10,000 words, the kanji to 2,000, and the grammar includes patterns from classical Japanese that appear in literature, legal documents, and formal writing. The listening section includes rapid, complex speech on abstract and specialist topics. N2 tests whether you can function in Japanese; N1 tests whether your Japanese is close to a native speaker's literacy level. The jump from N2 to N1 is significant — most learners need another 1–3 years and a major increase in immersion with advanced native material.
