Core 46

清音 46 characters

Tap any card to hear the pronunciation. Katakana shares the exact same sounds as hiragana — only the shapes are different. Once you know hiragana, katakana is mostly about learning to recognise a new set of forms for sounds you already know.

Katakana is used for foreign loanwords (コーヒー coffee, テレビ television), foreign names and places, scientific names, onomatopoeia, and for stylistic emphasis. It appears constantly in everyday Japanese — menus, signage, packaging, and text messages all use it heavily.

Foreign Sounds

外来語の音

Katakana can represent sounds that don't exist in standard Japanese by combining characters in new ways — usually a consonant character with a small vowel. These appear in loanwords and foreign names. They are not used in hiragana.

The long vowel mark ー extends any vowel for one extra beat. It only appears in katakana (hiragana spells out long vowels with an extra vowel character). コーヒー (kōhī, coffee) has two long vowels — the dash after コ and after ヒ each hold the previous sound. Cutting them short is one of the most common pronunciation errors.

Reading Guide

読み方のガイド

Katakana vs hiragana — when each is used

The two syllabaries cover the same sounds. The choice between them is determined by word origin and context, not pronunciation. Hiragana is for native Japanese words and grammar. Katakana is for:

  • Foreign loanwords — テレビ (TV), パソコン (computer), カフェ (café)
  • Foreign names and places — アメリカ (America), ロバート (Robert)
  • Scientific and technical terms — species names, chemical names
  • Onomatopoeia and sound effects — ドキドキ (heart pounding), ザーザー (heavy rain)
  • Emphasis — like using bold or italics in English

Easily confused pairs

Some katakana characters look very similar. The most common sources of confusion:

  • ソ (so) and ン (n) — both diagonal strokes, but the angles differ. ン leans more vertically.
  • シ (shi) and ツ (tsu) — same strokes, different orientation. シ has strokes on the left side; ツ has them on the top.
  • ア (a) and マ (ma) — share the top horizontal stroke but differ in the bottom structure.
  • ウ (u) and ワ (wa) — similar shape; ウ has a horizontal top stroke.

The long vowel mark ー

A horizontal dash (ー) extends the previous vowel for one extra mora (beat). It's used only in katakana. コーヒー (coffee) = ko-o-hi-i, not ko-hi. Getting vowel length right is critical — スキー (skiing) and すき (like) are completely different words.

Small ッ (double consonant)

Small ッ doubles the consonant that follows it, creating a brief pause — exactly like small っ in hiragana. ベッド (bed) = be-d-do, not be-do. You hear the pause before the doubled consonant.

FAQ

Most people who already know hiragana can learn all 46 katakana characters in 3–7 days of focused study. The sounds are identical — you're only learning new shapes. The hard part is the similar-looking pairs (ソ/ン, シ/ツ). Daily reading practice with real katakana words (menus, product labels, signs) speeds this up dramatically.
Japanese has a limited set of syllable types — mostly consonant + vowel. English consonant clusters and final consonants get vowels inserted between them. "Strike" becomes ストライク (su-to-ra-i-ku). "McDonald's" becomes マクドナルド (Ma-ku-do-na-ru-do). The original word is still recoverable once you understand the rules, and it becomes automatic with practice.

Some animal sounds, plant names, and slang also commonly appear in katakana. And manga uses katakana extensively for emphasis and sound effects, regardless of word origin.
For reading and typing, no. For hand-writing Japanese (filling in forms, writing notes), yes — though most modern learners type rather than write. If you do want to hand-write, katakana is arguably easier than hiragana because the strokes are straighter and the shapes are more angular.
