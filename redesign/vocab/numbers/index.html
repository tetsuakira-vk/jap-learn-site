Native Counting

ひとつ・ふたつ

Japanese has a second, older number system derived from native Japanese words. These are used for counting objects without a specific counter (up to ten), and appear in everyday speech. Beyond ten, the Sino-Japanese system takes over completely.

Large Numbers

大きい数

Japanese groups large numbers in units of ten thousand (万, まん), not thousands. This is the most common stumbling block for English speakers — 100,000 is not "one hundred thousand" but "ten man" (十万, じゅうまん).

Telling the Time

時間

Hours use the counter 時 (じ) and minutes use 分 (ふん/ぷん). Several hours and minutes have irregular readings — particularly 4 o'clock (よじ not よんじ), 7 o'clock (しちじ not ななじ), and 9 o'clock (くじ not きゅうじ).

Dates & Calendar

日付

Dates in Japanese go from largest to smallest: year → month → day. Days of the month have many irregular readings — the first ten days are almost all irregular. Japanese also has its own calendar era system (元号, げんごう): the current era is 令和 (Reiwa), which began in 2019 (令和元年).

Money

お金

Japanese yen (円, えん) has no decimal — there are no coins smaller than 1 yen. Prices are written with the ¥ symbol or with 円 after the number. Because of the 万 unit, large prices are chunked differently: ¥10,000 is 一万円 (いちまんえん), not "ten thousand yen."

Reading Guide

数字のポイント

Two systems, one language

Japanese has two number systems. The Sino-Japanese system (いち、に、さん…) is used for almost everything: counting, prices, time, phone numbers, measurements. The native Japanese system (ひとつ、ふたつ、みっつ…) is used informally for counting objects up to ten when no specific counter applies, and appears in some fixed expressions. For everyday use, master the Sino-Japanese system first.

Counters change the pronunciation

When a number is followed by a counter, the sounds often blend and change. This is called rendaku (連濁) or consonant mutation. The most common pattern is that 1, 6, 8, and 10 often change the following consonant — for example:

  • 一本 → いっぽん (not いちほん) — h becomes p after っ
  • 六本 → ろっぽん — same pattern
  • 三本 → さんぼん — h becomes b after ん
  • 一冊 → いっさつ — gemination of s

These patterns feel arbitrary at first but become intuitive with exposure. Learn them through vocabulary (一本のペン, 三枚の切手) rather than abstract rules.

The 万 unit

English counts in thousands: 1,000 / 10,000 / 100,000 / 1,000,000. Japanese counts in ten-thousands (万): 千 (1,000) / 一万 (10,000) / 十万 (100,000) / 百万 (1,000,000). When reading Japanese prices or figures, mentally re-chunk the digits in groups of four from the right, not three.

Zero

Zero has two readings. ゼロ (zero) is the loanword from English, used in modern contexts like phone numbers, scores, and temperatures. 零 (れい, rei) is the traditional Sino-Japanese form, used formally and in some fixed expressions. Both are correct; ゼロ is far more common in everyday speech.

Reading years

Japan uses both the Western year (西暦, せいれき) and the Japanese imperial era system (元号, げんごう). The current era is 令和 (Reiwa), which started May 1, 2019 = 令和元年 (Reiwa gannen — "first year of Reiwa"). 2026 = 令和8年. Official documents often use the Japanese era; everyday speech uses both interchangeably.

FAQ

Both mean 4, but context determines which to use. し (the Sino-Japanese reading) is avoided in some contexts because it sounds like 死 (death) — so in hospitals, hospitals avoid room number 4, and when counting objects you'll often hear よん instead. For the month April, however, し is standard (四月 = しがつ). For hours, 4 o'clock is よじ, never しじ. When in doubt, よん is the safe choice in everyday speech.
Like 4, しち can be confused with 一 (いち) over the phone or in noisy environments, so なな is often preferred for clarity. 7 o'clock is しちじ (hours use the Sino-Japanese set), but when counting items or saying phone numbers, なな is safer. July is しちがつ. You'll hear both in native speech — context and speaker preference both play a role.
Add 番目 (ばんめ) after the number: 一番目 (first), 二番目 (second), 三番目 (third). This works for any ordinal position. A quicker way is just 番 alone — 一番 means "number one" and also "most" or "best" (一番好き = I like [it] the most). For rankings and positions, 第〜 is more formal: 第一 (the first / No. 1), 第二 (the second).

Native speakers use the correct counters naturally, but they'll understand you regardless. Prioritise: 本 (long objects), 枚 (flat objects), 人 (people), 台 (machines), 匹 (small animals), 冊 (books), 杯 (drinks) — these cover the vast majority of daily situations.
