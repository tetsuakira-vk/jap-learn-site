丁寧さのレベル

Formal, casual, and everything between

Japanese has a built-in politeness system called keigo (敬語). For most learners, the important distinction is between formal speech (〜ます/〜です endings) and casual speech (plain form). Phrases marked Essential are the ones to learn first; Formal phrases are safe in any situation; Casual ones are for friends and family only.

すみません — the Swiss army knife phrase

すみません (sumimasen) does a lot of heavy lifting in Japanese. It means "excuse me" (to get someone's attention), "I'm sorry" (for minor inconveniences), and "thank you" (when someone has gone out of their way for you). When in doubt in a social situation, すみません is almost always safe.

ください vs お願いします

Both mean "please give me" or "please do" but at different politeness levels. 〜をください (kudasai) is the standard way to order or request something at a shop or restaurant. 〜をお願いします (onegaishimasu) is slightly more formal and is often used in service contexts or when making a bigger request. Either works in most situations — お願いします sounds a touch more polished.

The magic of ね and よ

Sentence-final particles change the tone without changing the meaning. ね (ne) seeks agreement or softens a statement — like "right?" or "isn't it?". よ (yo) adds assertion — "I'm telling you." そうですね (I see / you're right) is one of the most useful listener responses in Japanese conversation. Learning to sprinkle these in naturally makes your speech sound far more fluent.

When you're lost for words

Three phrases that buy you time and goodwill: ちょっと待ってください (please wait a moment), もう一度お願いします (please say that again), and ゆっくり話してください (please speak slowly). Japanese people generally appreciate any effort to speak Japanese and will almost always try to help.

すみません (sumimasen) is the go-to for minor apologies and getting attention — bumping into someone, asking a waiter, interrupting a conversation. It's also used to express gratitude when someone has troubled themselves for you. ごめんなさい (gomen nasai) is a more heartfelt apology, used when you've genuinely done something wrong. In casual speech, ごめん (gomen) alone is common between friends. For most tourist/daily situations, すみません covers almost everything.
Bowing accompanies many phrases naturally, but there's no strict rule. As a foreigner, any bowing gesture is appreciated and warmly received — even a small nod is fine. The depth of bow corresponds to the formality: a slight nod for casual thanks, a deeper bow (30°+) for formal apologies or meeting someone important. Don't stress about getting it wrong — the words matter more, and making the effort at all is what counts.
Say すみません (sumimasen) clearly and raise your hand slightly. Many restaurants now have call buttons on the table — press those if available. It's not common to snap fingers or shout across the room. Some establishments have staff that come by regularly; in that case, making eye contact and raising a hand is sufficient. In ramen shops and conveyor-belt sushi places, you may order directly via tablet or slot machine — no flagging needed.
